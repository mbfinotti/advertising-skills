## Corvid Analytics — attribution & incrementality: what's actually broken, and why MMM is not the next move

### Diagnosis 1: Google + LinkedIn claiming 118 for 74 demos is not a mystery — it's expected

Every platform's "conversions" number is generated by that platform's own pixel/click-ID, on that platform's own attribution window and model (often last-click, sometimes with a view-through component). Neither platform knows the other exists. A single prospect who saw a LinkedIn ad, later clicked a Google search ad, and then booked a demo gets counted as **one full conversion by each**. Summed, platform-reported numbers will *always* overshoot true CRM demos once you run more than one paid channel — this isn't measurement error, it's structural double-attribution. 118/74 = 60% overclaim, which is high but not exotic for two channels both running always-on, especially if LinkedIn's conversion window is long (LinkedIn defaults to 30-day click / 7-day view) and overlaps with branded Google Search.

**This means: stop using platform-native conversion counts to decide budget allocation.** They were never designed to be summed across platforms. The fix is deduplication at the lead level, not a smarter attribution model layered on top of numbers that are already double-counted.

**Fix (build once, cheap):**
1. Capture `gclid` (Google) and LinkedIn's click ID on every landing page, first-touch, into a hidden form field.
2. Persist it through to the CRM lead record (custom fields: `first_touch_source`, `first_touch_click_id`, `all_touch_sources` as an array/string).
3. Build one canonical, deduplicated touchpoint table: CRM demo → list of unique channels that touched that lead pre-conversion. Now "118 vs 74" becomes a real number — e.g. maybe 74 demos have 51 single-Google touches, 14 single-LinkedIn touches, 9 both.
4. Apply a simple rules-based multi-touch model (U-shaped or position-based) on *that* deduplicated table for reporting. This is a spreadsheet/SQL job, not a platform purchase.

This alone should resolve most of the 118-vs-74 gap and give leadership a defensible, non-inflated view of "which channel touches demos" — though note this still isn't causal (see below).

### Diagnosis 2: a steady 9% unexplained gap is a plumbing leak, not noise

If it were measurement noise you'd expect it to wander month to month. Steady-state residuals that survive every explanation you've tried are almost always one specific structural leak, not "attribution is fuzzy." Before considering any modeling investment, rule out in this order (cheapest checks first):

- **Click-ID loss**: Safari ITP / link-decoration stripping, ad blockers, or a redirect hop that drops `gclid`/LinkedIn's `li_fat_id` before it reaches your CRM form. Check what % of demos have *no* click ID captured despite coming from a paid session in your analytics — that % is a strong candidate for your 9%.
- **LinkedIn Lead Gen Forms**: if any LinkedIn spend uses native lead-gen forms (in-app, no landing page visit), the click ID often doesn't propagate to CRM the same way a landing-page conversion does. Easy to check, commonly the exact size of a small steady leak.
- **Brand-search cannibalization / halo**: paid brand search "capturing" demand that would have converted organically anyway, showing up as a Google-attributed demo that's really brand equity from LinkedIn/other awareness spend. This doesn't create a CRM/ad-platform gap per se, but it does explain why platforms overclaim relative to true incremental effect — relevant context for the next section.
- **Untagged sales-assisted or referral paths**: SDR outbound, customer referrals, or in-person events that get manually logged with a generic "inbound" source by reps, when they actually originated from an ad touch that just wasn't captured.

Run this as a two-week data audit before spending money on anything. At $70k/month total spend, this audit costs an analyst's time, not a platform contract.

### Diagnosis 3: what leadership actually wants — incrementality — needs an experiment, not a smarter attribution model

Multi-touch attribution (even done perfectly, fully deduplicated) tells you what channels were **present** in a converting path. It cannot tell you what would have happened **without** that channel — i.e., it can't tell you if LinkedIn is driving demos or just riding along on demand Google would have captured anyway. That's the actual question leadership is asking ("which platform actually drives demos"), and only a causal experiment answers it.

The CFO has ruled out geo holdouts and dark regions. That rules out geo-lift, which is the classical approach — but it does **not** rule out the platform-native incrementality tools, which don't turn anything off anywhere:

- **Google Ads Conversion Lift**: randomizes at the *user/auction* level — a held-out cohort sees a public-service/ghost ad instead of yours, in the same geographies, same time, same budget pacing. Nothing goes dark, spend keeps running exactly as the CFO wants. Needs a minimum conversion volume Google will tell you when you set it up (worth checking — 74/month total might be tight for a single-channel study; may need to run 2-3 months to get a read).
- **LinkedIn**: native lift studies are thinner than Google's, but LinkedIn does offer brand/conversion lift via a third-party partner (Nielsen) for the same user-level-holdout mechanic if minimum spend/volume thresholds are met — worth a call with your LinkedIn rep to check eligibility at $70k/month blended spend.
- If neither platform's minimums are met on their own, a **switchback / time-based holdout at the campaign level** (e.g., alternate weeks on/off for a single non-brand campaign, not a whole platform or region) is a fallback that's still compatible with "ads keep running" at the account level, even if it's a slightly weaker design than a true concurrent holdout.

This is the actual "most rigorous next step": **a platform-native lift study**, run for 8-12 weeks, sized against your real conversion volume, giving you a causal incremental-demo number per platform that the CFO's constraint doesn't block.

### Should you buy an MMM platform? No — not now, possibly not ever at this scale

MMM is the wrong tool for the two problems you actually have, and it's oversized for your spend level:

- **It doesn't fix double-counting.** MMM ingests weekly spend by channel and demos as the outcome — it never touches the platform-vs-platform attribution overlap. You'd still need the deduplication work above regardless of whether you buy MMM.
- **It doesn't explain a steady 9% residual.** That's a data-plumbing question; MMM will just silently absorb it into the model's error term or misattribute it to whichever channel correlates with it.
- **Two channels spending on similar always-on schedules is close to MMM's worst-case identifiability problem.** MMM separates channel effects from week-to-week *variation* in spend. If Google and LinkedIn budgets move together (both flat, both scaled up/down for the same seasonal reasons), the model can't cleanly separate their coefficients — classic multicollinearity. You'd need deliberately staggered spend patterns over a long history to get a trustworthy split, which is itself a kind of experiment design the CFO hasn't approved.
- **$70k/month is thin for MMM.** Commercial MMM platforms (Analytic Partners, Nielsen, etc.) are generally priced and staffed for spend in the high hundreds-of-thousands to millions per month, with 1-2+ years of weekly history across many channels to get workable statistical power. At two channels and ~12 data points a quarter of meaningful variation, credible intervals on the channel coefficients would likely be too wide to act on — you'd be buying false precision.
- **Open-source MMM (Meridian, Robyn) exists and is free**, but even that only becomes worth running once you have (a) clean deduplicated conversion data and (b) enough spend/channel variety that the regression has something to identify. Revisit MMM only if total spend scales past roughly $250-300k/month across 4+ channels with genuine budget variation over time, and even then, run it in-house with a contractor before signing an enterprise contract.

### Recommended sequence

1. **Weeks 1-2**: click-ID capture + CRM persistence fix; deduplicated touchpoint table. Resolves the 118-vs-74 illusion.
2. **Weeks 1-2 (parallel)**: data audit for the 9% leak — click-ID loss rate, LinkedIn lead-gen form gap, brand-search halo, rep-logged source accuracy.
3. **Weeks 3-4**: set up Google Ads Conversion Lift (check volume eligibility) and a LinkedIn lift study or partner-run equivalent; if minimums aren't met, fall back to a single-campaign time-based switchback.
4. **Weeks 4-16**: let the lift study run to a valid read (8-12 weeks minimum for statistical power at this volume).
5. **After that**: reallocate budget based on incremental-demo cost per platform from the lift study, not platform-reported conversions.
6. **Only reconsider MMM** if spend materially scales and you want a longer-run, cross-channel saturation view once the above is in place — and start with a free tool (Meridian/Robyn) before any commercial platform purchase.

The certainty leadership wants comes from step 3-4 (a real experiment), not from a bigger model bolted onto the same double-counted, leaky data you already have.
