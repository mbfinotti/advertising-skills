## Hook Standards Memo — Fact Check

**Verdict: none of the four rules should be codified as-is.** Two are real numbers stretched past what they support, one is a well-debunked myth, one has no credible source I can trace. Shipping this checklist Friday as written would embed bad rules in your creative process.

### 1. "0.25 seconds → logo must appear in the first quarter-second" 🔴 Reject
- I can't trace this to any credible study. It reads like a game-of-telephone mutation of the (already shaky) "you have 3 seconds" attention-capture claims.
- Even granting a sub-second attention window exists, "therefore the logo must be frame one" is a non sequitur — capturing attention and branding are different problems.
- It also contradicts actual platform guidance: Meta and YouTube creative-effectiveness research (thumb-stop ratio studies, ABCD framework) generally advise **against** leading with a logo, because it reads as "ad" and gets scrolled past. Brand is usually woven in by ~second 2-3 or revealed after the hook, not before it.

### 2. "85% of Facebook video is watched without sound" 🟠 Real number, wrong use
- This traces to a 2016 Digiday piece, widely recirculated since. It's a real, oft-cited figure — not fabricated.
- But it's decade-old, platform-specific, and was largely an artifact of Facebook's *default autoplay-muted* setting at the time, not a stable fact about human behavior.
- Sound-on culture has since shifted a lot (Reels, TikTok, Stories). Treating it as a current universal truth for "video" is outdated.
- The underlying design principle survives even if the number doesn't: **design hooks to work with captions/visuals alone.** Keep that rule, drop the specific percentage.

### 3. "Average attention span is 8 seconds, down from 12 (less than a goldfish)" 🔴 Debunked myth — do not use
- This is one of the most thoroughly discredited stats in marketing. It traces to a 2015 Microsoft Canada consumer-insights report, whose own cited sourcing (Statistic Brain / NCBI) doesn't actually support the number.
- Attention researchers (e.g., psychologist Gemma Briggs, cited widely in BBC and other debunking pieces) have specifically refuted both the 8-second figure and the goldfish comparison — "attention span" isn't a single fixed quantity; it's task- and motivation-dependent, and no rigorous study measured a population average dropping from 12s to 8s.
- Putting this in an internal standards doc is a reputational risk if a client, journalist, or new hire with marketing-research background fact-checks it.

### 4. "1.7 seconds per piece of content → hooks longer than 1.7s fail" 🟠 Real number, invalid conclusion
- This one has a real origin: Facebook's own research (circa 2016) reported ~1.7s average time spent per piece of mobile News Feed content (vs. ~2.5s desktop). It's dated and platform-specific, but not invented.
- The conclusion doesn't follow from the premise. 1.7s is an *average* dwell time across all content, including content that fails to stop the scroll. A good hook's entire job is to **beat that average**, not conform to it — Meta's own creative guidance talks about capturing attention within the first few seconds of a *video*, not enforcing a hard ceiling derived from average feed scroll speed.
- A literal "1.7 seconds or it fails" rule would force you to cut hooks that are working *because* they hold attention longer than typical content — the opposite of the goal.

## What to tell the team

- Kill rules #1 and #3 outright — no defensible source, #3 actively debunked.
- Keep the *intuitions* behind #2 and #4 (design for sound-off, front-load value fast) but strip the specific numbers and the false thresholds.
- Don't build a checklist around a single "hook length" or "logo timing" number pulled from one dated stat — platform, format, and objective (view-through vs. click vs. brand recall) all change what "works."

## A more defensible checklist skeleton

1. **Sound-off comprehension** — hook must communicate its core idea via visuals/captions alone; verify by watching muted.
2. **Pattern interrupt in first 1–3s** — visually or narratively distinct from typical feed content (not a fixed millisecond target).
3. **Delayed or woven-in branding** — logo/brand cue present but not blocking the hook; test both early and delayed placement per format.
4. **Relevance signal fast** — viewer should know "is this for me" within the opening beat, without requiring a specific stopwatch cutoff.
5. **A/B test hook variants per platform** — thumb-stop rate (Meta) / average view duration & APV curve (YouTube) as the real success metrics, not a borrowed time constant.
