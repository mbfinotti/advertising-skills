## Are the four rules solid? No — three are false, one is real but wrongly used. Here's the source-by-source breakdown, then a checklist you can actually defend by Friday.

### The four claims, checked against primary sources

**1. "0.25 seconds to capture attention, so the logo must appear in the first quarter-second" — false, and the fix is backwards.**
The number traces to a 2016 Fors Marsh study, but it measured *recall*, not attention capture: people could recognize feed content at statistically significant rates after 0.25 seconds of exposure. It is not an attention deadline, and it says nothing about logos. Worse, opening on a logo is a documented anti-pattern — it signals "this is an ad" at the exact moment the viewer decides whether to leave, spending your highest-attention seconds on the advertiser instead of the viewer. If anything, this rule should be inverted for cold-traffic ads.

**2. "85% of Facebook video is watched without sound" — unconfirmed, and doesn't need to be true anyway.**
This is a 2016 Digiday report of a handful of publishers' self-reported numbers (LittleThings, Mic). Facebook never confirmed it. You don't need this stat to justify sound-off design — Meta and LinkedIn autoplay muted by default, which is a verified platform mechanic on its own. Cite *that*, not the folklore number.

**3. "Average human attention span is 8 seconds, down from 12" — debunked, drop it entirely.**
No credible primary source exists for this claim (it's often mis-traced to a Microsoft Canada report that never said this). There's nothing to cite here; don't put it in a memo that has to survive scrutiny.

**4. "1.7 seconds per piece of content, so anything longer fails" — real number, wrong conclusion.**
Facebook did publish this as a 2016 average time-spent-per-content figure. But it's a decade-old average, not a threshold, and researchers (Nelson-Field, Field) argue 1.7 seconds is actually *below* the time needed for reliable brand encoding — memory only reliably encodes after roughly 2.5 seconds of active attention, though distinctive brand assets used well can bring that down to ~1.5s (Amplified/VCCP, 2025, 20,000+ views across 72 ads). Treating 1.7s as a hard ceiling would reject hooks for being too long based on a stat that argues the opposite: many hooks need *slightly more* time than 1.7s to actually land.

### Why this matters beyond pedantry
All four numbers share the same failure pattern: single old snapshot, unconfirmed source, stated as a universal law, then converted into a bright-line pass/fail rule. If this checklist ships and someone traces the citations, the whole document's credibility goes with it — and more concretely, rule #1 would make you *worse* at cold-traffic hooks by forcing exactly the pattern that kills them.

### What to put in the checklist instead

Replace the four rules with these, all traceable to sourced, current evidence:

- **Design for muted playback, not a sound-off percentage.** Meta and LinkedIn autoplay muted — verified platform behavior. First frame + on-screen text must carry the full promise; audio adds texture only.
- **Land the value signal by ~second 2, not "0.25 seconds."** Feed attention decays steeply — ~80% of viewers present at second 1, ~50% at second 2, ~20% at second 3 (Nelson-Field/Amplified Intelligence). The hook should make "what's in it for me" legible while most of the audience is still there — full first line, not a fractional-second logo flash.
- **Don't open on the logo for cold/DR traffic.** It's a named anti-pattern for direct-response. For brand/memory objectives (more relevant to a skincare brand building recall), weave in distinctive brand assets (color, product-in-hand) early rather than a logo card — that can work in as little as 1.5s per the VCCP data, but as *distinctive-asset* branding, not a logo stamp.
- **Set the hook window by placement, not a universal number.** Feed/Stories: ~3 seconds. YouTube skippable in-stream: ~5 seconds (to the skip button). Bumper/non-skippable: no scroll to stop — score continuity and branding instead, not "attention capture."
- **Gate on sound-off legibility, audience qualification, and promise-payoff continuity** — not an arbitrary duration ceiling. A hook can run longer than "1.7 seconds" and still win if it's specific and qualifies the right buyer; a hook can be instant and still fail if it stops the wrong viewer or the rest of the ad doesn't deliver what it promised.

### One process note
Where any of this came from a conference talk, ask for the *primary study*, not the slide. All four original claims are widely recirculated exactly because they sound authoritative on a slide with no citation — that's the actual pattern to build a checklist rule against: **no stat goes into the standards doc without a traceable primary source and a date.**
