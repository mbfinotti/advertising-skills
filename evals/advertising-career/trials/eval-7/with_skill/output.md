# Scenario round prep — paid social manager, DTC supplements brand

All three questions are "metric-diagnosis" format — the skill's evidence says interviewers here are screening for a **structured diagnostic order stated out loud**, not a list of possibilities and not a generic answer read off a template. Below: the order to name, then a spoken answer you can rehearse close to verbatim. Assumption baked in: DTC supplements on paid social means Meta/TikTok as primary channels, likely a subscription component (LTV matters), and iOS ATT under-reporting is live in every ROAS conversation. Flag if any of that's wrong and I'll adjust.

---

## Q1 — "Account ROAS dropped 30% over the last three weeks — walk us through your diagnosis."

**Say the order out loud before you dive in** — it signals process over guessing:

> "I'd check these in order of hit-rate per minute spent checking, not just likelihood: creative health, then audience saturation, then site-side, then attribution. Creative and audience are visible in the account in minutes; site-side and attribution mean pulling in other teams, so I check them last even though they're sometimes the real cause."

**1. Scope the window first.** "Three weeks is enough to rule out noise, but I'd still confirm this against the same three weeks last month and against a longer trailing average — supplements skew seasonal (New Year, summer-body, back-to-school), so I want to know if this is a real drop or a comp problem before I touch anything."

**2. Creative health (check first — causes ~60-70% of drops).** "I'd pull frequency and the age of the top-spending ads. Supplements audiences are relatively narrow and retargeting-heavy, so creative fatigues fast — if frequency's climbed and CTR/CVR on the winners has decayed, that's usually it. I'd check whether the creative testing cadence slowed in the same window — a lot of ROAS drops trace back to the team just not shipping new hooks."

**3. Audience saturation.** "Rising CPMs against flat or shrinking reach on the core segments. For a supplements brand this often shows up as: purchasers and lookalikes are tapped out, and the algorithm's spending into colder, less-qualified inventory to hit budget — same mechanism as the third question below."

**4. Site-side conversion.** "Did anything change on the landing page, offer, subscription pricing, or checkout in that window? Out-of-stock on a hero SKU is a classic supplements-specific one — traffic looks fine, conversion craters. The ad account can't fix a site problem, so I'd loop in whoever owns the DTC site before touching bids."

**5. Attribution/tracking.** "Did the CAPI setup, consent banner, or attribution window change? I'd check this last precisely because it's the one people jump to first and it's rarely the answer — but if nothing else lines up, this is where I'd look."

**Close on the business metric, not the platform number:** "Whatever I find, I'd report it against blended MER or contribution margin, not just platform ROAS — with iOS ATT, platform-reported ROAS on a subscription supplements brand can move independently of what's actually landing in the bank, especially once you factor in first-purchase vs. subscription LTV."

*Why this reads senior:* names the order, scopes the window before diagnosing, and closes on blended MER instead of vanity-metric ROAS — the skill flags "CTR/CPC talk without CPA/ROAS/MER" as a mid-level tell, and the fix here is the same instinct one level up: don't stop at platform ROAS either.

## Q2 — "An A/B test shows a 5% lift at p=0.08 — what do you do?"

Don't answer "significant" or "not significant" — that's the trap. Answer with a decision frame.

> "p=0.08 means I can't call this statistically significant at a standard 95% threshold — but the real question isn't the p-value in isolation, it's what it costs to be wrong in each direction, and whether the threshold was even set before the test ran."

**Then walk the frame:**

- "If this was a creative or copy variant — cheap to keep running, easy to reverse, low downside if the 'lift' turns out to be noise — I'd ship it and keep collecting data post-launch rather than block on significance. The cost of a false positive here is low."
- "If it's something expensive or hard to reverse — a bidding strategy change, a landing page rebuild, a pricing test on a subscription SKU — I'd extend the test to reach the pre-agreed power/duration rather than call it early. Calling a coin-flip result on an irreversible decision is the actual mistake, not the p=0.08 itself."
- "I'd also ask what the significance threshold and minimum runtime were set to *before* the test launched. If nobody set one, that's the process gap — not the 0.08."

**One line that lands well if asked to just decide:** "Given it's presented with no context on reversibility, I'd default to extending the test rather than shipping or killing — but tell me what the variant actually changes and I'll give you a real answer, not a rule of thumb."

*Why this reads senior:* the skill is explicit that naming "the threshold should have been set before the test" is the tell interviewers listen for — it shows you think about test design, not just test results.

## Q3 — "We doubled a winning campaign's daily budget from $2K to $5K and ROAS fell while CPM nearly doubled — why?"

This is close to a textbook question, so answer it precisely and don't over-hedge.

> "That's audience saturation — diminishing marginal returns on the buying audience, not a broken campaign. At $2K/day the algorithm was spending inside your efficient core: your best-converting, cheapest-to-reach supplement buyers and lookalikes. At $5K/day it has to find that much more inventory every day, so it reaches past the efficient core into colder, more competitive, more expensive placements and users — CPM goes up because you're now bidding against more advertisers for scarcer attention within that audience, and ROAS falls because the incremental users it's buying convert worse."

**What I'd do about it, in order:**

1. "Check frequency and reach by segment before anything else — if reach barely moved and frequency spiked, that confirms saturation rather than, say, a tracking or landing-page issue that happened to coincide with the budget change."
2. "I wouldn't jump straight back to $2K — I'd step the budget up gradually (e.g. 20% every 3-4 days) instead of a 2.5x jump, which gives the algorithm's learning phase time to actually find efficient incremental volume instead of dumping into broad reach overnight."
3. "In parallel, I'd widen the *supply* of good audience rather than just the budget — new creative angles, expansion into adjacent lookalikes, a new prospecting segment — so the extra spend has somewhere efficient to go instead of spilling into low-intent inventory."
4. "If the goal is real scale, not just a budget line, I'd reframe the conversation from 'raise this campaign's budget' to 'where's the next efficient audience pool' — that's a creative and audience-expansion problem, not a bid-and-budget problem."

*Why this reads senior:* the skill flags this exact scenario and its expected answer as "audience saturation / diminishing marginal returns — the algorithm reaches beyond the efficient core to spend the larger budget." Landing that cleanly, then pivoting to a concrete remediation plan (gradual ramp + audience expansion, not just "lower the budget back"), is what separates a correct answer from a strong one.
