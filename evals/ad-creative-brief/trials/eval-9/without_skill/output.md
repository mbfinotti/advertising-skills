# Fernhollow — Paid Launch Campaign Brief

## Message & Evidence Sections

**Inputs used:** founder-directed angle ("vet-formulated science"), ~3,100 reviews across Amazon + site (aggregate proof, not individual quotes — none were provided to me), 12 competitor ad screenshots referenced but not attached to this task, so the competitor teardown below is a fill-in template rather than an analysis of specific creative. Brand-new ad account = no pixel data, no retargeting pool, no brand recognition. Every message decision below assumes 100% cold traffic at launch.

---

## 1. Positioning

**Single-minded proposition:**

> Fernhollow is the joint supplement a vet would formulate for their own dog — not a supplement company that hired a vet to sign off on it.

That distinction matters because "vet-formulated" is a crowded, weakly-substantiated claim in this category. The wedge isn't the word "vet" — it's specificity: named formulator, named credentials, a dosing rationale a skeptical pet owner can inspect. Generic "vet-approved" claims get scrolled past; a formulation you can audit gets believed.

---

## 2. Message Architecture

**Hero claim:** Vet-formulated. Not vet-endorsed.

**Three supporting pillars** (every ad should map to exactly one, not all three — cold audiences need one idea per exposure):

| Pillar                         | Claim                                                                           | Job                                                      |
| ------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------- |
| **A. Formulation credibility** | Built by a licensed vet, dosed at clinical levels, not proprietary-blend-hidden | Overcomes "just another Amazon supplement" skepticism    |
| **B. Outcome proof**           | 3,100+ owners report visible mobility change                                    | Overcomes "will this actually work" skepticism           |
| **C. Safety/trust**            | Third-party tested, transparent sourcing, no filler                             | Overcomes "is this safe to give my dog daily" skepticism |

Pillar A carries the founder's angle. Pillars B and C exist because "science" alone doesn't close a purchase for a first-time buyer with zero brand trust — it needs an outcome promise (B) and a safety reassurance (C) to convert, not just inform. Treat A as the wedge, B as the closer, C as the risk-reversal.

---

## 3. Message-to-Funnel Mapping

Since the account has no retargeting pool yet, structure the first 2–3 weeks as a **single cold-traffic funnel that front-loads credibility**, then layer sequencing once pixel data exists.

- **Scroll-stop (first 3 sec / headline):** Pillar A — credibility hook. This is what earns the click from a skeptical cold audience.
- **Body (next 10–15 sec / body copy):** Pillar B — the review volume, framed as an outcome, not a star rating.
- **Close (CTA / final frame):** Pillar C — safety/guarantee, to de-risk a first purchase from an unknown brand.

Do not run all three pillars as separate simultaneous campaigns yet — with zero historical data, split-testing full pillars against each other (not just creative variants within one pillar) is the fastest way to learn what's actually driving CAC before scaling spend.

## 4. Ad Hooks & Headlines

**Pillar A — Formulation credibility**

1. "Formulated by a vet. Not just approved by one."
2. "Most joint supplements pay a vet for a quote. Ours built the formula."
3. "The dosing math your vet would actually check."
4. "No proprietary blend. Every dose, listed."

**Pillar B — Outcome proof** 5. "3,100+ dog owners. One recurring word: 'again.'" 6. "The supplement dog owners keep re-ordering — not just trying once." 7. "[X]% of reviewers mention their dog moving easier within [Y] weeks." _(needs real figure from review export — see §7)_

**Pillar C — Safety/trust** 8. "Third-party tested. Nothing hidden on the label." 9. "What we left out of the formula matters as much as what's in it." 10. "Try it. If your dog's routine doesn't change, we refund it." _(only if a guarantee actually exists — confirm before using)_

**Format notes:** Pillar A performs best as static/carousel (label close-ups, formulator quote card). Pillar B performs best as UGC/testimonial video — a review number is a claim, a dog owner saying it on camera is proof. Pillar C works as a comment-reply or FAQ-style short.

## 6. Evidence Section

### 6a. Evidence stack — Formulation credibility (Pillar A)

Required before launch, ranked by strength:

- Formulator's name, license/credential, and a one-line bio (highest-converting single asset for this pillar)
- Ingredient sourcing + dosing rationale in plain language (why this ingredient, at this dose, not a proprietary blend)
- Third-party lab test / Certificate of Analysis, if available
- NASC (National Animal Supplement Council) seal, if enrolled — strong trust shorthand in this category
- Manufacturing standard (cGMP-compliant facility, etc.)

### 6b. Evidence stack — Outcome proof (Pillar B)

Pull from the 3,100 reviews, don't just cite the count:

- Aggregate rating + review count (baseline trust signal, weakest proof on its own)
- % of reviews mentioning mobility/energy/stairs/play — this is the number that should headline the ad, not the raw review count
- 3–5 verbatim quotes, prioritized for specificity ("jumps on the couch again," not "great product") — pull separately from Amazon vs. site since Amazon reviews carry third-party credibility, site reviews can be richer/longer
- Before/after behavioral detail if any reviews describe it (stairs, car jumps, morning stiffness) — this is more persuasive than a star rating and should be mined specifically
- Repeat-purchase rate / subscription retention, if available — stronger proof than review count for an outcome claim, since it's owners voting with their wallet after having lived with results

### 6c. Claim-to-evidence compliance mapping

Flag before creative goes live — this is a real legal exposure point, not a formality:

- "Vet-formulated" must be substantiable (an actual formulating vet on record) — if the vet only reviewed/approved the formula rather than built it, the copy needs to say that, not "formulated"
- Avoid disease/cure language ("treats arthritis," "cures joint pain") — stay in structure/function territory ("supports joint mobility," "supports comfortable movement"), standard practice for supplements not regulated as drugs
- Any percentage claim (e.g., "87% saw improvement") must trace to an actual number in the review data or a study — do not round up from a soft aggregate
- If a guarantee is used in ad copy, confirm it's live and matches the actual refund policy before the ad launches

## One-paragraph pressure-test (not a blocker — flagging, not re-opening research)

The founder's angle is a reasonable wedge for a cold, brand-new account because it gives the ad something concrete to say in the first 3 seconds — the risk is if "vet-formulated" turns out to be table stakes once the competitor grid (§5) is filled in. Keep Pillar A as the hook but not the whole campaign: the B/C pillars above are already built in as a hedge, so if formulation credibility doesn't outperform in the first data pull, the outcome-proof creative is ready to promote to lead without a re-brief.
