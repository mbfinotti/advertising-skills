# Short answer: No — the data doesn't support "vertical video doesn't work," it supports "this specific video has a hook/retention problem, and the hero video is just fatiguing normally."

## Why the "kill vertical video" conclusion doesn't hold

**1. You already have proof vertical video works for this brand.**
The hero video crushed it for three weeks. One format, two outcomes (one great, one bad) means the variable is the creative, not the format. "Format doesn't work" requires ruling out creative quality first — you haven't.

**2. The new video's own numbers point to a specific, fixable failure — not a format failure.**

- 3‑sec view rate 31% is actually fine — roughly in line with typical Meta/TikTok hook benchmarks (20–30%+). The hook is stopping thumbs.
- Hold to 15s at 4% is the real signal: of people who watched 3 seconds, ~87% bailed before 15s. That's a **promise-payoff mismatch** — the hook sets up an expectation (curiosity, problem, visual) that the next 12 seconds don't deliver on. Classic causes: slow pacing after the cold open, branding/logo slammed in too early, message doesn't arrive until second 10+, or a jump-cut/tonal shift that breaks the hook's spell.
- CTR 0.3% and near-zero purchases are downstream of that collapse — if 96% of people leave before 15s, there's barely anyone left to click. This is consistent with a broken middle, not a broken medium.

**3. The hero video's fade is a separate, ordinary phenomenon: creative fatigue.**
Day 26, frequency 2.1 on cold prospecting, decaying results — that's the textbook lifecycle of a winning creative, not evidence against video as a format. Frequency 2.1 isn't even high; on a narrower/smaller cold audience that's enough to start seeing diminishing returns as reach saturates. The fix for fatigue is refreshing the creative (new hooks/cuts of the same proven concept) or widening/rotating audience — not abandoning the format that produced three weeks of strong performance.

**4. Comparing to the static campaign doesn't isolate "format" as the cause.**
Static and video ads usually run different messages, different funnel roles (video often serves cold awareness/hook, static often serves consideration/retargeting), and different audiences. "Static converts, video doesn't" could just as easily mean the video's message/offer clarity is worse, or the video is being shown to a colder, less bought-in audience — not that motion content underperforms stills for this brand.

## What's actually going on (most likely)

- The new video has a **hook that doesn't match its payoff** — strong stop, weak follow-through.
- The hero video is **fatiguing on a narrow cold-prospecting pool**, a frequency/audience-refresh problem, not a video problem.
- The team is pattern-matching two unrelated failures into one narrative ("video = bad") because it's a simpler story than "we have two different, fixable problems."

## What to do instead of moving everything to carousels

1. **Diagnose the new video's 3s–15s drop directly.** Watch it against the drop-off curve: what happens right after second 3? Test whether captions/sound-off comprehension, pacing, or a mistimed brand reveal is killing retention.
2. **A/B test hooks, not format.** Keep body/CTA constant, swap only the first 3 seconds, to isolate whether the hook is over-promising or just weak.
3. **Re-cut the hero video's proven hook with fresh footage/pacing** rather than retiring it — you're fighting fatigue, not failure.
4. **Expand or rotate the cold-prospecting audience** for the hero concept to reduce frequency pressure before touching creative.
5. **Check the post-click experience for video traffic specifically** — if even the 0.3% who click convert at zero, confirm the landing page matches the intent/temperature of someone coming from a 15-30s video vs. a static ad.
6. **Keep carousels as a complement, not a replacement.** They're a reasonable addition for consideration/retargeting stages, but swapping out video entirely on this evidence would mean discarding your only format that's produced three straight weeks of strong results.

Bottom line: the data describes a broken hook-to-payoff sequence in one video and normal fatigue in another — not a format-level verdict. Fix the middle of the new video and refresh the hero creative before concluding vertical video is off the table.
